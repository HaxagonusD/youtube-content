EDUCATIONAL VIDEO STORYTELLING FRAMEWORK
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

I'm writing an educational video. Help me apply this
5-section framework. Ask me clarifying questions before
drafting, especially about the topic, audience, and the
specific misconception I'm correcting.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
THE 5 SECTIONS (in order)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

1. HOOK         — open a curiosity gap (3–10 seconds)
2. FRAME        — stakes + scope, the contract (10–20s)
3. TENSION      — surface the misconception (15–45s)
4. BUILD        — stack of beats, each teaching one idea
5. PAYOFF/LAND  — close the loop + next move (20–40s)

The engine running underneath: open a curiosity gap in
section 1, widen it in section 3, close it in section 5.
Cut anything that doesn't widen or close that gap.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. HOOK
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A hook is a precisely-sized, specific information gap,
opened in under 10 seconds, that the viewer can't easily
close without watching the rest of the video.

Based on Loewenstein's information gap theory: curiosity
peaks when the viewer has *some* knowledge but not all —
not zero context (alien) and not full context (boring).
Aim for the Goldilocks zone.

Hook types (pick one):
  - Question hook       — pose the unanswered question
  - Counterintuitive    — state something that contradicts
                          their assumption
  - Cold open           — drop into a result/scene first,
                          explain after
  - Stakes frame        — name what they lose by not knowing
  - Puzzle/paradox      — two facts that seem to contradict
  - Promise             — name the transformation
  - Story open          — character in a moment, cut before
                          resolution

Hook killers: pre-explaining, hedging, generic framing,
resolving too fast, channel intros before the gap.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
2. FRAME
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Frame = the contract with the viewer. Two pillars:

STAKES — why this matters. Pick one flavor:
  - Practical          (they can do something new)
  - Cost-of-ignorance  (not knowing this is hurting them)
  - Identity           (changes how they see the field)
  - Curiosity payoff   (the answer itself is the reward)

  Formula: [specific outcome] + [for whom] + [why it
  matters now]

SCOPE — what's in and out. Four dimensions:
  - Audience           (who this is for)
  - Prereqs            (what I'm assuming you know)
  - Coverage           (what's in / out)
  - Time               (how long the video is)

Scope acts as an "advance organizer" (Ausubel) — it
creates a mental scaffold the viewer hangs every later
fact on. Without it, the teaching is a pile; with it,
each fact has a slot.

1–3 sentences total, ~10–20 seconds of video.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
3. TENSION
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

NOT story tension (will the protagonist win?).
This is COGNITIVE tension — the friction between what the
viewer thinks they know and what's actually true.

Based on Derek Muller's PhD research (Veritasium): clear
explanations alone fail because viewers map them onto
their existing wrong mental models and walk away
unchanged. You have to surface the misconception first
and let them feel the conflict before resolving it.

His finding: "Clarity numbs the mind, but confusion
can crack it open." Videos that incorporated misconceptions
produced ~9.1pt post-test gains vs ~6.8pt for traditional
explanations.

Tension shows up as one of:
  - Misconception        (they believe X; actually not-X)
  - Counterintuitive     (the obvious answer is wrong)
  - Hidden complexity    (looks simple, isn't)
  - Apparent contradiction (two true things conflict)
  - "Why is this hard?"  (admit the topic is weird upfront)

To find tension in any topic, ask:
  1. What does the viewer probably already believe?
  2. Where is that belief wrong/incomplete?
  3. Where does a beginner go "wait, what?"
  4. Where did MY naive intuition fail when I learned it?

Beat structure: name the misconception → show it doesn't
hold → don't resolve yet, let the discomfort sit.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
4. BUILD (the body — stack of beats)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A BEAT = one complete idea, delivered in 30–90 seconds,
with this internal structure:

  SETUP  (5–15s)  — name the idea, pose mini-question
  SHOW   (15–60s) — example/analogy/demo/counterexample
  LAND   (5–15s)  — say what just clicked

A video is a stack of 2–8 beats (depending on length),
plus Hook on top and Payoff on bottom.

Beats per video length:
  60–90s short  →  1–2 beats
  3–5 min       →  2–4 beats
  8–12 min      →  4–6 beats
  15–20 min     →  6–8 beats

Teaching tools (use ONE per beat — mixing muddies it):
  - Concrete example   (abstract idea → real instance)
  - Analogy            (new concept → familiar concept)
  - Demonstration      (show it happening live)
  - Counterexample     (what happens when rule is broken)

Beat rules:
  - One idea per beat. If two, split it.
  - Stack in dependency order — beat 2 needs beat 1.
  - If you can cut a beat and the video still makes
    sense, it wasn't a beat. Cut it.
  - Write the LAND first — knowing the click you're
    aiming at sharpens the example.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
5. PAYOFF + LAND
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Three jobs, in order:

  CLOSE THE LOOP  (10–20s)
    Explicitly answer the hook. Return to the SAME
    example/question/scene you opened with. Symmetry
    is what makes it feel finished.

  CRYSTALLIZE     (15–30s)
    State the takeaway in ONE sharp, quotable line.
    The line a viewer would repeat to a friend.
    If you can't compress the video to one line,
    you don't have a thesis.

  NEXT MOVE       (10–20s)
    Two options, pick by content type:
      A. Hook forward (explainers/concepts) — surface
         a new question that arises FROM what they just
         learned. Must feel organic, not tacked on.
      B. Action prompt (tutorials/how-tos) — one
         specific, doable thing. "Refactor one component
         tonight," not "go practice."

Do NOT do "smash like and subscribe" before the payoff.
Deliver the payoff first; engagement is earned.

The last 30 seconds are prime real estate — only ~16%
of viewers reach them, and they're your highest-value
viewers. Don't waste them on credits.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CONSTRAINTS / DEFAULTS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

- Total length target: 5–10 minutes unless otherwise
  specified. Guo et al.'s MOOC study (6.9M sessions)
  found 6 minutes is the sweet spot for educational
  engagement.
- Tone: informal, talking-head energy beats polished
  lecture (same study).
- First 30 seconds is make-or-break. Hook + Frame must
  land before then. ~55% of viewers leave by 60s if
  the opening is slow.
- Each section has ONE job. If a line doesn't open,
  widen, or close the curiosity gap — cut it.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
WHAT I NEED FROM YOU
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Before writing anything, ask me:
  1. What's the topic / video idea?
  2. Who's the audience and what do they already know?
  3. What's the misconception or naive intuition I'm
     correcting? (If I can't answer this, push me until
     I can — without it the video has no tension.)
  4. What's the ONE crystallized line I want them to
     walk away with?
  5. Target length?

Then draft section-by-section, and let me approve each
section before moving to the next.
